The double helix

Two strands wind around each other like a twisted ladder. It's a right-handed spiral: each full turn holds about 10.5 base pairs and climbs 3.4 nanometres. The whole ladder is only 2 nm wide, roughly 50,000 times thinner than a hair.

  • 1953Watson & Crick, built on Rosalind Franklin's Photo 51
  • ~2 mof DNA packed in every human cell
  • 3.1 bnbase pairs in one copy of your genome
02 / 11

The sugar-phosphate backbone

The rails of the ladder alternate deoxyribose sugars and phosphate groups, locked together by strong phosphodiester bonds. Each strand has a direction, 5′→3′, and the two run in opposite directions (antiparallel). The phosphates are negatively charged, which is why DNA moves toward the + electrode in a gel.

03 / 11

One nucleotide

Zoom in on one rung and you'll find the building block: phosphate + sugar + base. The bases come in two shapes. Purines (A, G) have two fused rings, a hexagon and a pentagon. Pyrimidines (C, T) have a single hexagon. Look for both shapes in the hologram.

  • PO₄phosphate, links one sugar to the next
  • C₅deoxyribose, a 5-sided sugar ring
  • A·T·G·Cthe base, which carries the information
04 / 11

The base-pair code

A always pairs with T, and G always pairs with C. A purine always meets a pyrimidine, so every rung is exactly the same width. That's why, in any DNA, the amount of A equals T and G equals C (Chargaff's rule).

05 / 11

Hydrogen bonds

The rungs are held together by hydrogen bonds, the faint dashes across the middle. A–T shares 2 and G–C shares 3, so GC-rich DNA takes more heat to separate. One bond is weak, but millions together are strong. The weakness is on purpose: the cell has to unzip DNA every time it reads or copies it.

  • 2H-bonds in every A–T pair
  • 3H-bonds in every G–C pair
  • ~5%of a covalent bond's strength
06 / 11

Major & minor grooves

The two backbones don't sit directly opposite each other, so the helix has a wide major groove (~2.2 nm) and a narrow minor groove (~1.2 nm). Proteins slip into the major groove and read the edges of the bases, so they can find a sequence without unzipping the DNA.

  • 2.2 nmmajor groove width
  • 1.2 nmminor groove width
  • ~137°angle between the two sugar attachments
07 / 11

Unzipping: replication

Watch the top of the helix split open. Helicase pries the strands apart at the replication fork, and DNA polymerase builds a new partner for each one, A for T and G for C. Every copy keeps one old strand and one new strand, which is called semiconservative replication.

  • ~50bases added per second at each fork
  • 1 in 10⁹error rate after proofreading
  • ~8 hto copy a human genome
08 / 11

Spools: nucleosomes

Two metres of DNA has to fit inside a nucleus 6 µm across. The cell's trick is spools. DNA wraps 1.65 times around a core of 8 histone proteins, about 147 base pairs per spool. Strung together they look like beads on a string, then coil tighter into chromatin.

  • 147 bpwrapped per nucleosome
  • 8histones in each core
  • ~30 Mnucleosomes in one cell
09 / 11

The chromosome

Before a cell divides, chromatin condenses about 10,000× into chromosomes. You have 23 pairs. Each one has already been copied into two identical sister chromatids, joined at the centromere, which gives the classic X shape. The stripes are G-bands, regions that pack differently.

  • 46chromosomes per human cell
  • 2sister chromatids per X
  • 1centromere pinching them together
10 / 11

What is a gene?

A gene is a stretch of DNA that holds the recipe for one product, usually a protein. Humans have about 20,000 protein-coding genes, yet together they make up only ~1.5% of the genome. The glowing section of the helix is one gene, HBB, which builds part of the hemoglobin in your blood. Keep scrolling to go inside it.
